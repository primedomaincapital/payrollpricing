How Long Does It Take to Switch Payroll Providers?

The controller of a 40-person logistics firm in Ohio made the decision on a Tuesday in October: the payroll provider had missed two tax deposits, and the company was leaving. She signed with the new vendor on Wednesday. Her first live payroll with them ran on December 19.

November 2026 · 8 min read

The controller of a 40-person logistics firm in Ohio made the decision on a Tuesday in October: the payroll provider had missed two tax deposits, and the company was leaving. She signed with the new vendor on Wednesday. Her first live payroll with them ran on December 19. In between, nothing dramatic happened, which was the achievement. Nobody missed a paycheck, no tax account went unregistered, and the only W-2s the staff received in January came from one company. The switch took nine weeks from signature to first live run, and the reason it took nine weeks is the reason most switches take the time they do: payroll is not a software migration. It is a tax migration with a software problem attached.

Ask five vendors how long a switch takes and you get five answers, all of them true for somebody. Gusto, which publishes the most candid guide in the industry, puts the range at two to six weeks, depending on headcount, complexity, and the two providers involved. Everee, writing in October 2026, gives the same two-to-six-week range and breaks it down week by week. QuickBooks agrees: about two to six weeks for most small businesses. Baron Payroll, a bureau that runs migrations for a living, puts the typical range at three to six weeks. Rise, which handles mid-year moves, puts mid-year switches at four to eight weeks. Warp, a newer vendor that runs the migration itself, says most of its customers go live in one to two weeks, with complex moves taking two to three. The honest range, across the market, is two to six weeks for a straightforward switch, four to eight for a mid-year move with several states and pay groups, and one to two only when a vendor does the work and the data is clean.

What the weeks are spent on

Week one is discovery and paperwork. The new provider needs the employee roster, pay rates, pay schedules, deduction and benefit elections, garnishments, time-off balances, and the tax registration numbers for every state where the company has employees. It also needs the contract position with the old provider: the notice period, the cancellation fee, and the date the old provider stops filing. The single most common delay in week one is the old contract: a 60-day notice clause discovered the week a company tries to leave, and the switch waits for the clause, not for the software.

Weeks two and three are data and tax accounts. Year-to-date payroll figures have to move across exactly: gross wages, taxes withheld, employer taxes paid, and deductions taken, quarter by quarter, employee by employee. The new provider registers, or confirms registration, for every state unemployment and withholding account. At the start of the year there is almost no year-to-date data to move. Mid-year, the new provider inherits six months of history, and if any of it is wrong, the year-end forms are wrong with it.

Week three or four is configuration: pay rules, overtime rules, shift differentials, bonus and commission handling, and the integrations with time tracking, benefits, and accounting. ADP, Paychex, and Rippling all run this as a structured implementation with a named specialist; Gusto and the smaller vendors run it as guided self-service with support on call. The difference is not speed. It is who catches the error: a specialist who has run the same configuration a hundred times, or the customer, on the first live run.

Then comes the step that decides whether the switch works: the parallel run. The new system runs a full payroll alongside the old one, for one or two pay periods, and the two outputs are reconciled line by line: gross pay, net pay, every deduction, every tax line, for every employee. Everee’s October 2026 guide puts the parallel run in weeks four to five of a six-week plan. QuickBooks calls it an essential operation before go-live. Rise calls it the single most important step in a mid-year migration. A parallel run that reconciles to the cent is the only proof the new system is ready.

The last stage is the quiet one: the first live payroll, then closing out the old provider. The old provider issues final filings for the periods it covered, and the company confirms, in writing, the date its filing responsibility ended. The new provider takes the quarters after that date. One missed handoff here produces the classic failure of a mid-year switch: two providers each believing the other filed a quarter, and a notice from the IRS months later proving neither did.

The banking delay nobody budgets for

One stage has grown longer in the last few years, and buyers who planned on the old timeline get caught by it. Before a payroll provider can move money from a company’s bank account to its employees, the banking system has to verify the account, a process that used to take two or three days and now routinely takes seven to 10 days, according to Baron Payroll’s December 2025 account of the process, because fraud prevention checks have tightened across the industry. A switch planned to go live in two weeks can lose half its runway to a check nobody on the sales call mentioned. Start the verification in week one, before the data work is finished.

Notice periods are the other quiet delay. A 30-day notice clause is common; 60 days is not rare. A company that signs with a new vendor on November 1, with a 60-day notice clause, cannot close the old provider out until the end of December, and a January 1 cutover becomes the plan by arithmetic rather than by choice. Check the clause before choosing the cutover date, not after. The contract is the timeline, and the implementation plan is the decoration.

The windows where switching goes wrong

There are three good moments to switch and one bad one that recurs every year. The best is January 1. No year-to-date data moves, the new provider files the whole year, and each employee gets a single W-2. Gusto’s own guide says it plainly: at the end of the year you do not have to transfer historical payroll data at all. The next best is the first day of a quarter, April 1, July 1, or October 1, because payroll taxes are filed quarterly on Form 941, which is due April 30, July 31, October 31, and January 31, and a quarter boundary gives the new provider a clean filing period. The worst is the last two weeks of December, when every vendor’s implementation team is booked solid with year-end work, W-2s are being prepared, and a mistake made in the rush is discovered in January, when the correction is a filed form rather than a draft.

The year-end window has a trap of its own. Companies that wait for January 1 to escape a bad provider spend October, November, and December enduring the provider they have already decided to leave. Three months of missed deposits or wrong deductions, endured for the sake of a clean cutover, can cost more than a mid-year switch would have. A mid-year switch that is reconciled properly produces one W-2 per employee, not two, because the new provider imports the year-to-date figures and files the year as one record. The fear of dual W-2s is the most common reason companies delay, and it is a solved problem: Warp, Rise, and Gusto all describe the same mechanism, a full year-to-date import with a single consolidated year-end form.

Quarter-end has its own edge. Switching the week a quarterly return is due means two providers, one deadline, and a filing owned by exactly one of them. Cut over the day after a quarter closes, never the week a return is due, and confirm in writing which provider files that quarter.

The counterargument: switch whenever, or not at all

The case against waiting is strong. If the current provider is missing deposits, the cost of staying is a federal penalty of 2% of the shortfall at one to five days late, rising to 10% past 15 days, and the penalty is charged to the employer, not to the vendor, unless the contract says otherwise and the vendor honours it. Waiting three months for a clean January cutover while deposits go missing is not prudence. It is a penalty accruing on a schedule. The providers that promise a 48-hour migration are usually selling the same logic in the other direction: the switch is simple, the data will move, sign now. A provider that promises a full migration in two days with no dedicated onboarding is describing a risk, not a feature, and the risk lands on the first payday after go-live.

The case against switching at all deserves a hearing too. Every switch carries a failure mode the current provider has already survived: the new system’s first quarter is its least tested, and the old system’s errors are at least known errors. A company whose complaint is price, not accuracy, may be better served renegotiating than migrating. The switch is worth it when the current provider gets the compliance wrong. It is a poor trade when the current provider merely charges more than the buyer likes.

Both objections point at the same test. Switch when the compliance is broken, and switch on a quarter boundary. Stay and renegotiate when the complaint is price alone. And never let a vendor’s timeline replace your own: the vendor’s one-to-two-week promise assumes your data is clean, your contract is already out of notice, and your bank verification clears on the first attempt. If any of those three is false, the honest timeline is four to eight weeks, and planning for two is how a switch becomes a story the staff tells for years.

What a clean switch looks like

Start eight weeks before the cutover you want, not two. Read the old contract first and calendar the notice date before choosing the new provider. Ask the new vendor, in writing, who loads the year-to-date data, who registers the state tax accounts, and who owns the parallel run. Run the parallel for at least one full pay period and reconcile it to the cent before the first live run. Confirm in writing which provider files which quarter, and keep the old provider open until the first live payroll has cleared and the first tax deposit has posted.

The Ohio logistics firm did all of that, and its nine-week switch is the unremarkable outcome a switch is supposed to produce: a new provider, the same payday, and nobody noticing. The companies that notice a switch are the ones that compressed it into a fortnight because a vendor said it could be done, skipped the parallel run because the data looked clean, or cut over in the last week of December because the calendar said January 1 was close enough. Payroll is the one system in a company where a quiet transition is the only good review. Two to six weeks, spent in sequence, is what a quiet one costs.
