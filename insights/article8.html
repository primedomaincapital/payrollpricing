Payroll Tax Penalties: What the IRS Actually Charges for Late Filings

The notice arrived on a Thursday in May, addressed to a 60-person manufacturing company in Georgia, and it was for $4,800. The deposit it concerned had been $48,000, made 20 days late because the bookkeeper who ran payroll was in the hospital and nobody else knew the login.

November 2026 · 8 min read

The notice arrived on a Thursday in May, addressed to a 60-person manufacturing company in Georgia, and it was for $4,800. The deposit it concerned had been $48,000, made 20 days late because the bookkeeper who ran payroll was in the hospital and nobody else knew the login. The company’s owner read the number twice. Ten percent of the deposit, for being 20 days late, on money the IRS had already received. He paid it, then asked the question that should have been asked the day the deposit was missed: what does the IRS actually charge, and how bad can it get?

The answer is a schedule, not a judgment. The Internal Revenue Service publishes it, the statute sets it, and the numbers are smaller than most owners fear and larger than most owners expect, because there are three separate penalties, they run at the same time, and interest runs on top of all of them. A single missed deposit can produce one bill. A missed deposit plus a late return plus an unpaid balance can produce three.

The deposit penalty: four tiers, no stacking

The first penalty, and usually the largest, is the failure-to-deposit penalty under Internal Revenue Code Section 6656. It applies to the amount of the deposit that was late, not to the company’s total tax bill, and it is set by how many calendar days late the money was. One to five calendar days late costs 2% of the unpaid deposit. Six to 15 days costs 5%. More than 15 days costs 10%. And if the deposit is still unpaid more than 10 days after the IRS sends its first notice, or on the day the IRS demands immediate payment, the rate rises to 15%.

Three features of the schedule catch people out. The days are calendar days, not business days, so a deposit due on a Friday and made on the following Monday is three days late, not one. The tiers replace each other rather than adding up: a deposit more than 15 days late carries a 10% penalty, not 2% plus 5% plus 10%. And the 15% tier is not about how late the deposit was. It is about ignoring the IRS after the notice arrives, which is why it is the most avoidable rate on the schedule.

Run the Georgia company’s numbers through it. A $48,000 deposit, 20 days late, is past 15 days, so the rate is 10% and the penalty is $4,800. Had the deposit been made on day five, the penalty would have been $960. Had it been made on day 15, $2,400. A $20,000 deposit shows the same shape at smaller scale: $400 at two percent, $1,000 at five, $2,000 at 10. The penalty is a clock, and the clock is priced in thousands.

The filing penalty and the payment penalty

The second penalty is the failure-to-file penalty under Section 6651, and it applies when the quarterly return, Form 941, is late. The rate is 5% of the unpaid tax required to be shown on the return for each month, or part of a month, that the return is late, up to a maximum of 25%. Five months late reaches the cap. A return more than 60 days late also carries a minimum penalty, reported for returns due after December 31, 2025 as $525 or 100% of the tax owed, whichever is less. Form 941 is due on the last day of the month after each quarter ends: April 30, July 31, October 31, and January 31, with 10 extra calendar days to file if all deposits for the quarter were made on time.

The third penalty is the failure-to-pay penalty, also under Section 6651, at 0.5% of the unpaid tax per month or part of a month, capped at 25%. It runs while the tax stays unpaid, which is why it keeps growing long after the filing penalty has hit its cap. Where both the filing and the payment penalty apply in the same month, the filing penalty is reduced from 5% to 4.5% for that month, so the combined rate for the month is 5%, not 5.5%. Run out over the full period, the combined ceiling for the two penalties is 47.5% of the unpaid tax: 22.5% for filing plus 25% for paying late.

Put the three together on one example. A company owes $50,000 in tax for a quarter, files three months late, and pays three months late. With the offset, the filing penalty is 4.5% a month, or 13.5%, which is $6,750, and the payment penalty is $750, for a combined $7,500 on a $50,000 liability, before interest and before any deposit penalty.

Interest is separate from all three penalties, and it never stops while a balance remains. Under Section 6621 the underpayment rate is the federal short-term rate plus three percentage points, it compounds daily, and the IRS resets it every calendar quarter. For the fourth quarter of 2026 the rate was 7% a year for underpayments, under IRS release IR-2026-98 and Revenue Ruling 2026-15. Interest is also charged on the penalties themselves, not only on the tax. A year at 7% costs roughly $3,500 in interest on $50,000.

The penalty that follows the owner home

All of the above is charged to the business. The Trust Fund Recovery Penalty, under Section 6672, is charged to a person. When a company withholds income tax and the employee’s share of Social Security and Medicare tax from wages, that money is held in trust for the United States. If it is not paid over, the IRS may assess a penalty equal to 100% of the unpaid trust fund taxes against any responsible person: an owner, an officer, or even a bookkeeper with check-signing authority who knew the taxes were unpaid and paid other creditors instead.

One hundred percent is the whole of the withheld amount, and it is personal. The company’s limited liability does not shield it, and the penalty can be assessed against more than one responsible person for the same tax, with the IRS collecting the total once. An owner who pays the supplier before the IRS, knowing the deposit is due, has made a decision with personal consequences rather than a cash-flow choice.

The same logic reaches the year-end forms. For information returns due in 2026, the penalties run $60, $130, or $340 per form depending on how quickly the error is corrected, and $680 per form for intentional disregard, with no cap. A company with 60 employees that files its W-2s late and corrects them slowly can owe $20,400 on the forms alone, before any tax penalty.

The counterargument: the penalties are survivable, and the first one is often free

There is a case that this schedule is less frightening than it reads. The deposit penalty is charged on the shortfall, not on the payroll, so a company that deposits most of what it owes on time pays a penalty on the gap, not on the total. The filing penalty is charged on the unpaid tax, so a company that deposited on time and filed late owes little or nothing under it. And the first penalty is frequently removed altogether. First Time Abate is the IRS’s administrative waiver for a taxpayer with a clean compliance history: no significant penalties for the same type of tax in the prior three years, all required returns filed, and the tax itself paid or arranged. It covers failure to file, failure to pay, and failure to deposit, regardless of amount, and it is applied before the IRS considers reasonable cause. From summer 2026 the IRS has been moving this relief to an automatic system, the Automatic Exemption from Penalty, under which qualifying penalties for 2025 returns and later are not assessed at all, without a request.

That case is mostly right, and it is dangerous precisely because it is mostly right. First Time Abate is a one-period waiver, and it rests on history rather than on the facts of what went wrong. A business that uses it for a first-quarter miss has spent its only automatic removal, and if a real problem arises in the next three years, the safety net is gone. The waiver removes the penalty, never the tax or its interest. And the automatic exemption, as reported in 2026, is a transition in progress rather than a settled system: the IRS announced it in November 2025, and practitioners were still reporting a phased rollout through 2026.

Reasonable cause is the fallback, and it is narrower than its name suggests. The standard is whether the taxpayer exercised ordinary business care and prudence and was still unable to meet the obligation. Fires, natural disasters, the death or serious illness of the person responsible, destroyed records, and system failures can qualify. Reliance on a tax professional, lack of knowledge, mistakes and oversights, and lack of funds by itself generally do not.

What to do in the first 48 hours

The order of operations after a missed deposit is short and unforgiving. Pay the deposit immediately, through the Electronic Federal Tax Payment System, because the penalty clock runs in calendar days and every day moves the rate. File the return, even if the tax cannot be paid in full, because the filing penalty is 5% a month and the payment penalty is 0.5%, and filing stops the larger of the two. Then check First Time Abate eligibility before drafting a reasonable-cause letter: if the prior three years are clean, the penalty may be removed by a phone call to the number on the notice, or by a written request on Form 843, Claim for Refund and Request for Abatement. If First Time Abate is not available, build the reasonable-cause case with dates and documents, and keep the tax itself paid or under an installment agreement, because interest runs either way.

For a company that discovers the miss before the IRS does, the calculus is simpler still. Paying on day six instead of day 16 saves 5% of the shortfall. Paying on day 16 instead of day 60 saves nothing on the rate, but it stops the interest and keeps the 15% tier out of reach. The cheapest day to fix a missed deposit is today.

The Georgia company paid its $4,800, called the IRS, and had the penalty removed under First Time Abate, because its prior three years were clean and the hospital stay was documented. It changed the process: two people now hold the payroll login, the deposit calendar is shared, and a missed deposit alerts the owner the same day. The penalty was survivable. The lesson was not that the IRS is forgiving. It was that a single missed deposit, priced at 10%, is cheap compared with the one that follows it, priced at 15%, with interest, against a person.
