The True Cost of Payroll Errors: Penalties, Amendments, and the $291 Mistake

The W-2 went out with the wrong Social Security number. One digit, transposed by a tired administrator in January. By the time anyone noticed, the IRS had flagged the mismatch, the employee’s tax return was rejected, and the company was facing correction penalties on top of the hours spent untangling it. Total cost of a single transposed digit: just under a thousand dollars.

Here are the twelve to watch for.

That is not an anecdote. It is the statistical norm. Ernst & Young’s payroll research found that roughly one in five payrolls contains an error — an 80.15% accuracy rate across surveyed companies — and the weighted average cost per error is $291: $281 in direct costs like overpayments and remediation, plus $10 in indirect labor. Twelve errors at that average costs $3,492. And that is before the IRS gets involved.

The penalty ladder

Payroll tax penalties are tiered by lateness, and the tiers are designed to hurt more the longer you wait. Miss a federal tax deposit by one to five days: 2% of the unpaid amount. Six to fifteen days: 5%. Beyond that: 10%. If the money is still unpaid ten days after the IRS’s first notice, the penalty jumps to 15%. Daily interest on unpaid balances currently runs at 7% for 2026.

The W-2 correction penalties are their own special pain. For tax year 2025 filings, the IRS charges $60 per form corrected within 30 days, $120 by August 1, and $340 after August 1 — per form, per section. When both Section 6721 (filing) and 6722 (furnishing) penalties apply to the same error with no relief, the combined exposure reaches $680 per affected employee. Intentional disregard starts at $680 per form with no cap. Yearli’s 2026 analysis models the stacking: a single W-2 error corrected after August 1, combining both penalty sections with EY’s $291 average error cost, totals $971 per employee. Intentional disregard: $1,651.

Then there is the ACA employer mandate, which operates on a different and larger scale. For 2026, the Section 4980H(a) penalty — failure to offer coverage to substantially all full-time employees — is $3,340 per employee per year ($278.33 per month), minus the first 30 employees. The (b) penalty for unaffordable coverage is $5,010. For a 200-employee company that stumbles on the (a) penalty, the annual exposure is $567,800. These are not rounding errors. They are business-threatening numbers.

Why errors keep happening

The uncomfortable truth is that payroll has gotten harder, not easier, even as software has improved. IRS Notice 2025-62 created a one-year grace period for 2025 on the new overtime-and-tips reporting breakdown — employers will not be penalized for failing to report the specific premium breakdown on 2025 forms, provided total wages are correct. Starting January 1, 2026, separate reporting becomes mandatory, with penalties of $60 to $680 per incomplete form. Every employer running payroll in 2026 is implementing new reporting logic for the first time, under penalty.

Worker classification remains the perennial trap. The IRS’s automated matching systems now cross-reference filings with third-party data, making it harder for misclassification to go unnoticed. A contractor reclassified as an employee triggers back employment taxes, penalties, and interest — retroactively.

And the error rate compounds with complexity. Multi-state payroll, equity compensation, garnishments, tip reporting, paid family leave programs that differ by jurisdiction — each layer adds failure points. EY found 14% of surveyed companies reported payroll-related litigation, averaging $3,200 in direct costs and 29 internal hours per incident. Another 14% reported regulatory or compliance issues, averaging $5,200 in fines, $4,600 in legal costs, and 91 internal hours. The $291 average hides a long tail of five-figure disasters.

The counterargument: software solved this, right?

Modern payroll platforms — Gusto, Rippling, ADP, Paychex — automate calculations, filings, and deposits. Surely the error rate should be approaching zero? The data says otherwise, and the reason is that software automates computation, not judgment. The platform calculates correctly on the inputs it receives. The errors live upstream: the wrong hire date entered by a manager, the bonus coded as regular wages, the terminated employee left active for one extra cycle, the state withholding election never updated after a move.

Automation has also created a new failure mode: confident errors at scale. A misconfigured earning code does not produce one wrong paycheck — it produces four hundred, across two pay cycles, before anyone notices. The manual era produced small, random errors. The automated era produces systematic ones. The $291 average is arguably too low for the modern failure mode, because when automation fails, it fails in bulk.

What the disciplined operators do

The companies with the lowest payroll error costs share three habits. First, they reconcile every cycle — not quarterly, not annually, every pay run — comparing gross-to-net, flagging variances above a threshold, and requiring sign-off before submission. Second, they calendar every filing deadline with a two-week buffer, because the penalty tiers punish lateness on a curve and the 30-day correction window is the cheapest place to catch mistakes. Third, they audit their own data quarterly: a sample of employee records checked against source documents, catching the transposed digits and stale addresses before they become corrections.

The deeper lesson is about where payroll sits in the organization. Companies that treat it as an administrative afterthought — the thing the office manager handles between other duties — pay the $291 average and the occasional $5,200 fine. Companies that treat it as a compliance function with real financial risk invest in the controls that keep the error rate down. The IRS does not grade on effort. It grades on accuracy, and the grading curve gets steeper every year.

For a 50-employee company, the math is stark. At a 20% error rate across 26 pay cycles, that is 260 errors a year. At $291 each, $75,660 in error costs — before a single IRS penalty. The payroll service costs a fraction of that. The reconciliation discipline costs almost nothing. The errors are the expensive part.
