What Does Payroll Cost Per Employee Per Month?

A bakery owner in Tampa with nine employees sat down with three payroll quotes and found three different answers to the same question. One vendor quoted $79 a month. Another quoted $200. The third would not quote at all until she had taken a sales call.

November 2026 · 8 min read

A bakery owner in Tampa with nine employees sat down with three payroll quotes and found three different answers to the same question. One vendor quoted $79 a month. Another quoted $200. The third would not quote at all until she had taken a sales call. All three were selling the same thing: paying nine people, on time, with the taxes filed. The gap between the cheapest and the most expensive was not a rounding error. It was the difference between a price and a pricing structure, and payroll vendors have learned to sell the structure while the buyer shops the price.

The honest answer is that there is no single number. There is a base fee, a per-person fee, and a set of add-ons that most buyers discover after they sign. The base fee is fixed. The per-person fee scales with headcount. The add-ons scale with everything else: how many states you operate in, how fast you need the money to move, and how much hand-holding you want when something goes wrong.

The published prices, headcount by headcount

Gusto is the rare major vendor that publishes its prices, and its November 2026 price card is the clearest benchmark in the market. Simple costs $49 a month plus $6 per person. Plus costs $80 a month plus $12 per person. Premium costs $180 a month plus $22 per person. Those are list prices from Gusto’s own pricing page, confirmed by multiple independent reviews that read the page in October and November 2026. There is no long-term contract; billing is month to month.

Run the arithmetic for a 10-person team and the bands separate fast. On Simple, 10 people cost $49 plus $60, or $109 a month. On Plus, $80 plus $120, or $200 a month. On Premium, $180 plus $220, or $400 a month. The same 10 people, the same payroll, a spread of $291 a month between the bottom and the top of one vendor’s own menu. Over a year, that is $1,308 against $4,800, before a single add-on.

At one employee, the base fee dominates. A solo owner on Simple pays $49 plus $6, or $55 a month. On Plus that same person costs $92; on Premium, $202. At five employees, Simple is $79 a month, Plus is $140, Premium is $290. At 25 employees, Simple is $199, Plus is $380, and Premium is $730. At 50 employees, Simple reaches $349 a month, Plus $680, and Premium $1,280. The pattern is the point: the base fee matters most to the smallest teams, and the per-person fee matters most to everyone else. A 50-person company pays nearly four times as much on Premium as on Simple, and the per-person rate, $22 against $6, does most of the damage.

Rippling starts from a different structure. Its published floor is $8 per user per month plus a $40 monthly base fee, and its United States payroll module starts at $8 per user per month on top of the core platform. Payroll cannot be bought without the core human-resources system, and every further module is quoted. Independent pricing guides put a typical Rippling payroll, HR, and IT setup at $20 to $35 per person per month all-in. A 10-person team therefore starts near $120 a month on paper and lands well above it in practice, once the modules a real company needs are switched on.

ADP, the largest payroll provider in the United States, publishes no prices at all. Its RUN product, aimed at companies with 1 to 49 employees, is sold in four bundles, Essential through HR Pro, and every one of them routes to a sales quote. Its mid-market product, Workforce Now, for 50 to 999 employees, is quoted the same way. Third-party reviewers who tracked RUN in October 2026 put the most commonly reported Essential figure near $59 to $79 a month plus about $4 per person, but ADP confirms none of it, other reports disagree, and ADP’s own terms allow processing fees to rise on 30 days’ notice once a new client’s first six months have passed. Paychex follows the same model: no published list, a quote after a conversation. The two biggest names in the industry are the two hardest to price without talking to them.

Where the bill doubles

The base fee and the per-person fee are the advertised cost. The real cost lives in the lines below them, and four of them do most of the damage. Geography is the first. Gusto Simple covers payroll in one state. Hire one employee who lives across a state line and the vendor moves you to Plus, doubling the per-person fee from $6 to $12 and raising the base from $49 to $80. A single remote hire can add $1,092 a year to a 10-person team’s bill before that person is paid a dollar.

Speed is the second. On Gusto Simple and Plus, same-day pay costs $90 per payroll run and instant pay costs $100 per payroll run. A biweekly employer that needs money to move fast pays that charge 26 times a year. Two fast runs a month is $2,160 a year, more than a 10-person team’s whole annual Simple subscription. The fee is optional, which is why it is easy to miss, and it is charged per run, which is why it compounds.

Support is the third. Gusto’s Priority Support add-on costs $30 a month plus $3 per person, according to a reviewer’s live account billing page from October 2026. For a 10-person team that is $60 a month, $720 a year, to get a faster answer from the company already being paid to run payroll. Health insurance administration adds $6 per eligible employee per month unless Gusto is the broker of record. Business compliance became an $85-per-month, per-state add-on on Simple and Plus in Gusto’s October 1, 2026 repackaging, which moved entitlements between plans without changing a single published rate.

The fourth is headcount itself, counted the vendor’s way rather than yours. Gusto charges its per-person fee for every active employee, even in months when that employee was not paid. A seasonal worker kept on the books through the off-season keeps costing $6, $12, or $22 a month. Contractors are charged only in months they are actually paid, which is the one place the counting rules cut in the buyer’s favor. A company with a long tail of inactive employees on file is paying for ghosts.

The cost that is not the software

None of this is the total cost of payroll. It is the software and service fee, and it sits on top of the employer’s share of payroll taxes, which dwarf it. Social Security costs the employer 6.2% of wages up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500, and Medicare costs 1.45% of every dollar, with no cap. Federal unemployment tax, FUTA, runs 6.0% on the first $7,000 of each employee’s wages, reduced by a credit of up to 5.4% for employers who pay state unemployment tax in full and on time, leaving an effective 0.6% and a maximum of $42 per employee per year. A 10-person team’s software bill of $109 to $400 a month is a footnote next to its tax bill, which is precisely why vendors can charge it: the buyer is already writing much larger checks, and the software line looks small by comparison. Errors add a further line: EY puts the average cost of a single payroll error at $291, before any IRS penalty, and one in five payrolls contains an error.

The counterargument: the fee is trivial, so stop shopping it

There is a serious case that this entire comparison is over-engineered. For a company paying 10 people an average of $60,000 a year, annual wages run $600,000 and employer payroll taxes add roughly another $46,000. Against that, the difference between Gusto Simple at $1,308 a year and Premium at $4,800 a year is about half of one percent of the wage bill. The cheapest plan is not obviously the right one if the dearer plan prevents a single late deposit, and a late deposit carries a federal penalty of 2% at one to five days late, rising to 10% past 15 days. A buyer who spends a week haggling over $6 a person and then misses a deposit has saved pennies and spent dollars.

That argument is right about scale and wrong about what it excuses. The fee is small relative to wages, but it is large relative to the other administrative costs a small company pays, and it is the one cost the buyer can actually negotiate before signing. The vendors know the fee looks trivial next to the wage bill, which is why the add-ons are priced the way they are: $90 here, $6 a person there, each too small to argue about, together large enough to matter.

There is also the opposite trap: buying the top tier for protection that never arrives. Premium’s dedicated support and compliance alerts are worth $291 a month over Simple only if the company uses them, and many small teams buy the top plan, never call support, and never open the compliance dashboard. For a multi-state team of 10 to 25, Plus is usually the right middle: the per-person fee doubles, but the single-state wall disappears and time tracking arrives with it. For a single-state team of five, Simple at $79 a month is hard to beat.

What disciplined buyers do

Price payroll the way the vendors price it: in two numbers, not one. Get the base fee and the per-person fee in writing, at your headcount, in your states. Then price the four add-ons that move the bill: multi-state coverage, payment speed, support, and benefits administration. Ask how inactive employees are counted, and whether the per-person fee is charged in months nobody is paid. Ask what happens to the price after six months, and get the answer in the contract rather than in a sales email. ADP’s 30-day notice clause after month six is the warning label for the whole industry: the introductory price is not the price.

Run the numbers at three headcounts, not one: where you are now, where you will be in a year, and where you would be if you hired two people in another state. A plan that is cheapest at five people is often not cheapest at 25, because the per-person fee, not the base fee, decides the bill as the team grows.

The bakery owner in Tampa chose the $79-a-month plan, in one state, with no add-ons, and wrote the four add-on prices inside the quote folder. She will need Plus the month she hires across the state line, and she will know it is coming, which is the whole advantage. Payroll pricing is not a mystery. It is a base fee, a per-person fee, and four quiet lines underneath, and the companies that read all six numbers before they sign never have to renegotiate them later.
